Why bonded execution

Between the guardrail that blocks and
the receipt that shrugs, there was nothing.

A blocking rule is only as good as the attack it anticipated — Bankr had rules, and a prompt injection walked past them by poisoning the input, not the output. A receipt proves what happened after the fact and offers no recourse. Warrant adds the third regime.

Regime 1 — the guardrail

Blocks what it anticipated

Policy engines, spend caps, allowlists. The rule inspects the action the model proposes — but a manipulated model proposes convincingly. Bypassed by construction the day the attack is novel.

Fails silently: a blocked attack leaves no evidence.

Regime 2 — the receipt

Proves, after the fact

Signed logs, notarized traces, attestations. Useful forensics — but it guarantees nothing before the loss and compensates nothing after it. The money is already gone.

No recourse: proof of the theft is not a refund.

Regime 3 — the warrant

Bets, and pays if it lied

The agent commits to the final onchain state before reading anything poisoned, with money behind the claim. An injection can redirect a transfer; it cannot make the end state match a commitment made earlier — written by the policy, never by the agent.

Failure becomes an onchain artifact — and the victim is paid.

How it works

Four steps, one signature, zero trust in the agent’s word

1

Quote — free

The agent submits the raw transaction it intends to run. Category and notional are derived from the calldata, never declared. The bond is priced clamp(min, riskBps × notional, max) — and doubt always costs the maximum.

2

402 — terms before money

One response carries both challenges: an x402 v2 PAYMENT-REQUIRED and an MPP WWW-Authenticate: Payment. Every term the agent must sign is published in the challenge itself.

3

Sign — fund & commit atomically

The EIP-3009 authorization’s nonce is constrained to equal the termsHash. Opening pulls the bond and records the commitment in a single transaction — nothing to intercept, nothing orphaned.

4

Execute & settle

KeeperHub executes with simulation and sponsored gas. An independent archive RPC re-reads the post-condition at a pinned block. Held → refund minus 2.5%. Violated → bond to the capital owner, verdict to ERC-8004.

nonce == termsHash

The trick that closes the loop: an EIP-3009 signature only covers who pays, whom, and how much — not what was agreed. By requiring the payment nonce to equal the hash of the warrant’s terms, one signature binds the payment and the mandate, and the token itself guarantees it can never be replayed. The contract exposes termsHash() so clients derive exactly what it expects.

Don’t trust — replay

A verdict document publishes the pinned block, the RPC, and every check with expected and observed values. Its keccak256 is what ERC-8004 committed to. You need cast, jq and a public RPC — no account, no API key, nothing of ours.

Payment

Two rails in, one warrant out

POST /v1/warrants answers its 402 with an x402 v2 challenge and an MPP challenge on the same response, and takes either one back.

RAIL A — x402 v2

The HTTP-native payment standard

The exact scheme already carries an EIP-3009 authorization — Warrant constrains its nonce and hands it to the escrow. Settled by the public facilitator on Base Sepolia.

RAIL B — MPP

WWW-Authenticate: Payment

Method evm from the MPP registry. The Credential’s transaction payload unwraps into the same x402 payload — challenges are MAC-bound, single-use, replay answered with 409.

Both rails carry the identical EIP-3009 authorization, so fundingRef — the nonce, which is the terms hash — is the same on either path. A conformance test asserts the two openings field by field, and an MPP-rail warrant was opened, executed and settled onchain end to end.
The escrow

One contract. No proxy, no governance,
no emergency exit.

Funds leave through honor, slash or reclaim — there is no fourth door. Audited against its own declared invariants; findings, proofs-of-concept and fixes are public in the repository.

INVARIANT I6

A slash pays the protocol zero

The full bond goes to the capital owner — the treasury cannot be the beneficiary, checked onchain. Whoever operates the settler has nothing to gain from slashing you.

INVARIANT I9

The settlement window closes

honor and slash revert after expiry. Past it, reclaim is permissionless: anyone can trigger the full refund. A failing settler can never hold a bond hostage — and no error path ever leads to a slash.

INVARIANT I10

Opening is not seizing

opener != settler, enforced in the constructor and both setters. Verified against the live contract: setSettler(opener) reverts RolesMustDiffer(). Compromising the component that opens grants no power to seize.

The limit, stated before you find it

“Warrant guarantees outcome conformance, not decision quality.”

Post-conditions are strictly onchain-verifiable: balance deltas, allowance, health factor, nonce, emitted events. Warrant does not judge the intent or the wisdom of a decision — that is undecidable, and claiming otherwise collapses at the first counterexample. This boundary is a documented design choice, not a gap.

FAQ

The questions worth asking

What actually happens under a prompt injection?

The injected agent still acts — Warrant doesn’t pretend to stop it. But the post-condition was committed before the poisoned content was read, and it is generated by the capital owner’s policy, not proposed by the agent. A diverted transfer commits to a destination that is not the attacker’s, so the commitment fails, the bond is seized, and the capital owner is made whole. We demonstrated exactly this onchain: 1 USDC sent to 0x…dEaD, slashed, reason written in plain text on the chain.

Who writes the post-condition?

The policy — never the agent. The agent submits only the raw transaction it intends to run. Category and notional are derived from the calldata; allowedDest comes from the policy’s allowlist; and the Gateway injects a calldata_matches_commitment check that cannot be supplied, removed or overridden by the caller. An agent under injection has nothing to lie about.

What if the settler goes down — or turns malicious?

Down: honor/slash close at expiry and reclaim is permissionless — anyone triggers the full refund, so a bond can’t be held hostage. Malicious: a slash pays the protocol zero (invariant I6, onchain), so there is nothing to gain; and every verdict must publish the pinned block and full checks, so a wrong slash is a divergence anyone can prove with the replay script.

What does it cost the agent?

The bond is clamp(minBond, riskBps × notional, maxBond) — an unclassifiable action costs the maximum, because a system where uncertainty lowers the price is a system you attack by manufacturing uncertainty. Honored: the bond comes back minus a 2.5% fee frozen at open. Slashed: the whole bond goes to the capital owner. Expired without settlement: refunded in full.

Why should I believe any of these numbers?

You shouldn’t — that’s the point. Every claim on this page resolves to a transaction hash on Base Sepolia or a document whose keccak256 is committed in the ERC-8004 registry. replay-verdict.sh re-runs the six checks from a public RPC alone. 57 of 57 published verdicts replay with no divergence; the day one diverges, that’s a finding, and the script will print it.
